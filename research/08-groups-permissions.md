# User-created audience groups and fine-grained sharing: what the research and users say

Research notes for Parlour (small self-hosted forum, ~500 members, ex-Discord). Question under study: should ordinary members be able to create their own member lists ("circles") and use them in the read/write permissions of subforums they own, including a personal feed? Compiled October 2026. Claims not traced to a primary source are marked *(unverified)*.

---

## 1. Google+ Circles

### Design rationale

Google+ Circles came directly out of Paul Adams' 2010 Google UX research deck "The Real Life Social Network" (224 slides, ~400k SlideShare views). Adams' thesis was that people have not one social graph but "a core group of 4-to-6 communities... where each is focused on an aspect of one's life, from work to BFFs" and that there is "an impedance mismatch between what you share on Facebook and your interactions in real life" ([summary at skritch.blogspot.com](https://skritch.blogspot.com/2011/07/paul-adams-real-life-social-network.html); [CNN Money, 2011](https://money.cnn.com/2011/07/19/technology/google_plus_facebook/index.htm)). Google "seized upon Paul's research and came up with the Circles product, which was to be the core differentiator between Google+ ... and Facebook" (ibid.). Notably, Adams left Google for Facebook before launch and later argued that Facebook's simpler model was right in practice *(unverified beyond press reports)*.

The model: a circle is "simply a named list of users" (Watson et al., below); following is **asymmetric** (A circling B does not require B to circle A); the person circled is notified they were added but **not** which circle. "When you add someone to a Circle, they do get a notification, but it only tells them that you added them on Google Plus. They cannot see what circle/s you added them to" ([MakeUseOf](https://www.makeuseof.com/tag/7-must-know-tips-about-managing-your-google-circles/)). Posts carried a visibility label, "Public", "Extended circles" or "Limited"; clicking "Limited" showed the list of people (not the circle names).

### Kairam, Brzozowski, Huffaker & Chi, "Talking in Circles: Selective Sharing in Google+" (CHI 2012)

[PDF](https://idl.cs.washington.edu/files/2012-SelectiveSharing-CHI.pdf). Log analysis of the field trial plus a survey (n≈165 responses) and interviews.

- Selective sharing was the dominant behaviour among early adopters: "about three quarters (74.8%) of users who share during the period engaged in selective sharing using Circles at least once... 67.6% of all items were shared using Circles and 33.8% [publicly]" (Table 1, one week in July 2011). Caveat: this was an invite-only field trial populated by enthusiasts.
- Circle names clustered on **life facets** ("work", "school"), **tie strength** ("close", "best", "inner"; "acquaintances", "extended") and topical interests. Interviewees described "Inner Circles" as "These people pretty much know the 'unfiltered' me" [P1].
- Reasons for limiting an audience were **privacy**, **relevance** (23% of reasons: "I didn't want to pollute their stream with things that probably won't interest them") and **social norms** (7.9%), and users also gave reasons for *widening* audiences. The authors' reframing: "Rather than framing decisions about privacy as 'boundaries' based on who is 'allowed' to view content, users may instead be balancing reasons to limit or to distribute content."
- On adoption: "While similar controls may be available on other networks (e.g. 'Lists' on Facebook), making them central to the sharing model may be key to widespread user adoption."
- On maintenance: "Users in our study often referenced future plans to modify Circles based on sharing or consumption challenges experienced during use, suggesting that the complexity of these real-life boundaries might make them difficult to infer automatically."

### Watson, Besmer & Lipford, "+Your Circles: Sharing Behavior on Google+" (SOUPS 2012)

[PDF](https://cups.cs.cmu.edu/soups/2012/proceedings/a12_Watson.pdf). 27 interviews, two rounds. The more sceptical follow-up:

- "many people are not using circles to protect sensitive disclosures but instead use them to direct relevant information to interested social groups."
- Comprehension was fine; usage was not: participants "were overwhelmingly able to articulate correct understanding (12/15 and 9/12)", yet "a number of participants (5/15 and 3/12) reported only rarely using them to selectively post content", and some "(2/15 and 4/12) placed all of their Google+ social connections in a single circle."
- The canonical maintenance-burden quote, P3: "I used to try and manage it, but it was a lot to think about... Do I post to friends? Do I post to family? But what if friends are in family category too? It just kind of got confusing so I just started posting to everyone under friends right now." She had built ~10 circles, then collapsed them to one.
- "even under the most favorable usability conditions, would some people consider it too much additional effort to selectively manage friend groups?" and "if site usage grows and users add more connections, the burden of managing circles is also likely to grow."
- Who-sees-what failures: "A final source of potential unintended disclosures may arise from the challenges of keeping track of who is in what circle... They may inadequately place people, or forget who is where, and thus share with unintended people." One participant (P19) believed she had posted publicly when she had not, and "noticed people she did not know in her friends circle and had no recollection of how they were added."
- Audience indicators were barely noticed: "only one third of our participants (6/15 and 3/12) had previously noticed the Limited or Public labels next to each post." Design recommendation: show the circles a post went to, not just a flat list of people, because the list "makes it difficult to recall how it was shared, and to notice if users can now view it who are outside any of their circles."
- Asymmetry: "Some participants mentioned that the unidirectional relationships could be confusing, but for the most part participants seemed to understand them... unidirectional relations seemed more lightweight and experienced less pressure than 'friending' on Facebook."
- Users "did not understand re-sharing and commenting, and how it can extend the post's original audience."

### Post-mortem opinions

Popular post-mortems converge on the same three failure modes: "categorizing people by circles and then also deciding which circle should receive what" was too much friction (HN user mojuba, [2025 thread](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=44926271)); "Alice could add Bob to a circle without Bob adding Alice back. Made it so much harder for the network effect to kick in" (buzzerbetrayed, ibid.); and "unless you really think about it, you don't know where that content is going to" ([em360tech](https://em360tech.com/tech-articles/what-happened-google-why-googles-social-circles-failed)). Several commenters note the idea survived elsewhere: group chats (WhatsApp) became the de facto circles, and niche communities (TTRPG players) "worked incredibly well" with circles (citizenkeen, HN). An older HN thread records the onboarding noise of asymmetric circling: "several hundred people will add you to their circles, and you will get notifications when that happens" ([HN 2012](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=3452912)).

**Takeaway:** people understood circles and liked the *idea*; the per-post decision cost and the growing, invisible membership bookkeeping killed it. Circles were used more for *relevance routing* than secrecy.

## 2. Facebook lists, context collapse, and what people actually do

### Usage of custom lists

Facebook's manually curated Friend Lists were "used by only 5% of the user base" and "95 percent of Facebook users hadn't even used a single list" before Smart Lists launched in September 2011 ([Adweek](https://www.adweek.com/performance-marketing/smart-lists/); repeated by [TechCrunch, 2018](https://techcrunch.com/2018/08/09/facebook-is-shutting-down-friend-list-feeds-today/)). Smart Lists auto-populated Family / Co-workers / Classmates / Local lists and added two curated ones, **Close Friends** and **Acquaintances**, plus a **Restricted** list that sees only public posts. In August 2018 Facebook killed Friend List *Feeds* (reading a list as a feed) "to focus on improving your main News Feed experience", while keeping lists as a posting audience ([Engadget](https://www.engadget.com/2018-08-09-facebook-nixes-friend-list-feeds-but-friend-lists-live-on.html)). Today the audience selector offers Public / Friends / Friends except... / Specific friends / Only me, i.e. the per-post ad-hoc exclusion ("Friends except") largely replaced curated lists.

Vitak (2012), survey of 364 Facebook users: "using the Friend List feature... which 17% of participants reported using." But the users who did use lists got real value: posts "sent just to users' Friend Lists were significantly more honest... intimate... detailed... and sincere" than their other posts, and "as the size and diversity of users' networks increased, they were more likely to use Friend Lists" ([Vitak 2012, JOBEM](https://pearl.umd.edu/wp-content/uploads/2017/08/Vitak-2012-JOBEM.pdf)). A 2012 ASONAM study ("How do Facebookers Use Friendlists") found "users' customized friend lists are less frequently created and have fewer overlaps as compared to Facebook created friend lists" and "users do not place all of their friends into lists" ([ACM](https://dl.acm.org/doi/10.1109/ASONAM.2012.63)).

Settings also fail silently: Liu, Gummadi, Krishnamurthy & Mislove (IMC 2011) found Facebook privacy settings "match users' expectations only 37% of the time", 36% of content was still on defaults, and "when privacy settings are incorrect, they almost always expose content to more users than expected" ([Semantic Scholar](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/740f4097ca7011a542766f35f2e9bd8064ca30b5)).

### Context collapse and the imagined audience

- Marwick & boyd (2011): "We may understand that the Twitter or Facebook audience is potentially limitless, but we often act as if it were bounded." Users coped with context collapse by self-censorship and "balance", not by tooling ([New Media & Society](https://psycnet.apa.org/record/2011-03777-008)).
- Hogan (2010) coined the **lowest common denominator** strategy: posting only what is appropriate for the most socially distant person who could see it ([Bulletin of Science, Technology & Society](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/abs/10.1177/0270467610385893)). Vitak's data pushed back: "contrary to Hogan's... users do not sterilize their accounts as their networks grow", but the minority who segment audiences post more intimately.
- Litt & Hargittai (2016), diary study of 119 adults / 1,200 posts: "In over half the posts, participants said they were addressing an 'abstract' audience of anyone... just under half the posts had a target audience in mind," most often "homogeneous and composed of people's friends and family", and "Users' imaginings typically fluctuated among these audience types as they posted even though the potential audience as per their posts' privacy settings often did not change" ([Web Use Project](http://webuse.org/p/a52/)). So the mental model is per-post and fluid; the settings are static.
- boyd & Marwick's **social steganography**: teens "hide messages in plain sight by leveraging shared knowledge"; "teens have given up on controlling access to content because it's too hard, too frustrating... Instead, they've been focusing on controlling access to meaning" ([Boing Boing summary](https://boingboing.net/2013/05/22/social-steganography-how-teen.html)). The parallel coping strategy is **multiple accounts** ("finsta", throwaways), i.e. separating audiences by *account* rather than by list, because an account boundary is legible and hard to get wrong.

### Fine-grained controls that did get used

- Facebook lists at 17% (Vitak) with measurable honesty gains for those users.
- Google+ circles at 75% among field-trial enthusiasts (Kairam), falling off as networks grew (Watson).
- Instagram **Close Friends** (one list, green ring) is the clear success story; see section 3.
- Dreamwidth/LiveJournal access filters, heavily used in fandom; see section 3.

Pattern: fine-grained controls get used where (a) the community is small enough to know, (b) there are one or two salient lists rather than many, and (c) the control is central to the posting flow rather than buried.

## 3. Other systems: Dreamwidth, Diaspora, Mastodon, Bluesky, Instagram, Snapchat, Twitter Circles

### Dreamwidth / LiveJournal

Dreamwidth separates two relationship edges: **subscribe** (read them) and **grant access** (let them read your locked posts), both one-way. On top of that, "Filters are custom groups. There are two types of filters: reading filters and access filters. Reading filters display a subset of your Circle on your reading page. Access filters restrict access to locked content to a smaller subset of your access list" ([DW FAQ 22](https://www.dreamwidth.org/support/faqbrowse?faqid=22)). Entry security is Public / Access List / Custom (tick one or more access filters) / Private ([DW FAQ 54](https://www.dreamwidth.org/support/faqbrowse?faqid=54)).

Visibility of membership is deliberately nil, inherited from LiveJournal: "the Custom icon and the list of the groups to which the entry is filtered are visible only to you. Members of your custom friends groups will not be able to see whether or to whom an entry has been filtered; to them, the entry will look the same as a Friends-only entry" ([LJ FAQ 102](https://www.livejournal.com/support/faq/102.html)). Fandom tutorials for Tumblr refugees stress: "You can set up as many access filters as you want and change them at any time, and none of the members are notified of these changes" ([dreamwidth-help tumblr](https://dreamwidth-help.tumblr.com/post/132011409667/cfmvirus-i-tried-to-make-a-tutorial-for-access)). Fandom etiquette posts describe asking authors to be added to a custom group to see locked fic ([fandom_flutter](https://fandom-flutter.livejournal.com/631.html)).

Strengths: the lock icon on every non-public entry gives readers a single honest signal ("not everyone can see this") without leaking the list; filters are the poster's private tool. Complaints *(from community posts, unverified as to prevalence)*: filter management lives on a separate page far from posting; people forget which filter someone is on; and a reader cannot tell whether a locked entry is "all access" or "a filter I happen to be in", which can cause accidental cross-talk in comments.

### Diaspora "aspects"

Same shape as circles, with explicit secrecy: "No one else will ever be able to see the names of your aspects, and your contacts will never know which aspect(s) they are in. That is your business alone." Sharing is asymmetric ("people who are sharing with you but with whom you are not sharing – your 'followers'") ([diasporafoundation.org](https://diasporafoundation.org/getting_started/aspects)). Diaspora never reached scale, so there is little usage data *(unverified)*.

### Mastodon and Bluesky

Mastodon has four coarse visibilities with icons: Public (globe), Unlisted/Quiet public (moon or open lock), Followers only (closed lock), Mentioned people only (@) ([fedi.tips](https://fedi.tips/who-can-see-my-posts-in-mastodon-how-do-i-send-dms-in-mastodon/)). Lists are **read-side only**; the request to post to a list ("Make Post/Toot available to selected List(s)", [issue #7182](https://github.com/mastodon/mastodon/issues/7182)) was closed as not planned. Followers-only is weak unless follow requests are on: "otherwise anyone could follow you just to see your followers-only posts" (fedi.tips). Forks (glitch-soc, Hometown) add a fifth "Local only" visibility: the *community boundary* as an audience. Bluesky has no private posts; the long-running request thread opens "I know people who refuse to use BlueSky because it doesn't have a way to privatize their account" (mayaarane), with staff (bnewbold) saying only "We are working to add support for shared, personal, and other non-public content in the protocol" ([atproto discussion #2809](https://github.com/bluesky-social/atproto/discussions/2809)). Interim shipped features were coarse: followers-only *replies* (Feb 2025) and opting out of Discover for non-followers.

### Instagram Close Friends, Snapchat private stories, Twitter Circles

Instagram launched Close Friends in Nov 2018 after 17 months of testing as **a single list per account**. Product director Robby Stein: "No one has ever mastered a close friends graph and made it easy for people to understand." "No one gets notified if they're added or removed from your list that only you can view." Audience indicator: a green ring on the story. "We're protecting you and your right to share or not share to certain people. It gives you air cover" ([TechCrunch](https://techcrunch.com/2018/11/30/how-instagram-close-friends-works/)). It was explicitly a productisation of finstas. Snapchat allows **multiple** private stories, each a hand-picked list, and viewers of a private story can see who else is in it *(unverified)*. Twitter Circles (2022-2023) was one list of up to 150; a bug in April 2023 surfaced Circle tweets publicly, Twitter admitting "a security incident may have allowed users outside of your Twitter Circle to see tweets that should have otherwise been limited" ([TechCrunch](https://techcrunch.com/2023/05/05/twitter-confirms-circle-tweets-temporarily-were-not-private/)), and the feature was removed in Oct 2023. TechCrunch's post-mortem: "internet folks have craved a place where they could share their most private thoughts with an audience of people they trust", and a single list removes the friction of running a second account ([TechCrunch 2023](https://techcrunch.com/2023/11/01/the-demise-of-twitter-circles-left-a-void-that-instagram-close-friends-cant-fill/)).

**Granularity evidence:** one or two named tiers (Close Friends; followers-only) get mass adoption. N named lists get adopted by a committed minority (fandom on DW, ~17% on Facebook). Many lists plus per-post choice (G+) collapse back to one.

## 4. Group-based permission models in chat/forum software

Which systems let a *non-admin member* define a group and then use it as an ACL on a space they own?

| System | Who creates groups | Group usable as ACL on member-owned space? | Membership visibility |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Zulip** (10.x+) | Configurable ("Who can create user groups"); never guests; Cloud requires paid plan ([help](https://zulip.com/help/manage-user-groups)) | Yes. Channel settings "Who can administer / subscribe / subscribe anyone / send messages / start new topics / move / resolve / delete" each take any mix of roles, groups and users ([channel permissions](https://zulip.com/help/channel-permissions)) | Groups are organisation-wide objects, members listed openly |
| **Discourse** | Admins only; group **owners** (set by admin) can add/remove members and approve requests ([meta guide](https://meta.discourse.org/t/configuring-group-access-membership-and-visibility/138296)) | Admin creates the category and sets Security (See / Reply / Create per group). Members cannot create categories | Per group: "Who can see this group?" and "Who can see this group's members?" (Everyone / logged-on / owners+members+mods / owners+staff) |
| **Slack** | Owners/admins by default; can be opened to all members; paid plans only ([Slack help](https://slack.com/help/articles/212906697-Create-and-edit-user-groups)) | No; user groups are for @-mention and default channels, not channel ACLs | Open |
| **Discord** | Admins/Manage Roles only; 250 role cap; self-assignable roles only via admin-configured Onboarding questions ([Onboarding FAQ](https://support.discord.com/hc/en-us/articles/11074987197975-Community-Onboarding-FAQ)) | Only admins build channel permission overwrites | Roles shown on profile |
| **Mattermost** | Custom groups: all members by default, restrictable to a "Custom Group Manager" role (v7.2+) ([docs](https://docs.mattermost.com/end-user-guide/collaborate/organize-using-custom-user-groups.html)) | Mention only; LDAP groups can gate teams/private channels (Enterprise) | Open |
| **Matrix** | No groups; a Space is a room, and the *restricted* join rule (MSC3083) lets a room admin say "members of space X can join" ([MSC3083](https://github.com/matrix-org/matrix-spec-proposals/blob/main/proposals/3083-restricted-rooms.md)) | Yes in effect: any user who can create a room can create a private Space, invite people, and restrict other rooms to it | Space membership visible to space members |

### Zulip

Zulip is the closest existing design to the Parlour proposal. Its March 2025 post describes a permission system where you can "configure the permissions you want, granting them to any combination of roles... groups, and individual users", "Express your organization's structure with groups", and nest groups: "*engineers* = *engineering-managers* + individual engineers" ([Zulip blog](https://blog.zulip.com/2025/03/20/flexible-permissions-management/)). Each group has its own meta-permissions, "Who can administer this group, Who can mention this group, Who can add members to this group, Who can remove members from this group, Who can join this group, and Who can leave this group" (`can_manage_group`, `can_add_members_group`, etc.; [API](https://zulip.com/api/update-user-group)). "A user who belongs to a subgroup of a group is treated as a member of that group" and "Guests can never administer user groups, add anyone else to a group, or remove anyone else from a group" ([help](https://zulip.com/help/manage-user-groups)). The design issues ([#25929](https://github.com/zulip/zulip/issues/25929), [#25942](https://github.com/zulip/zulip/issues/25942)) motivate splitting "manage" from "add members" so that a group can be self-service without being self-administered. Notably Zulip's default for "who can create user groups" is a configurable organisation setting rather than "everyone" *(the exact default is unverified; Cloud gates it behind paid plans)*, and Zulip's private channels are opaque even to admins: "organization administrators and channel administrators [who are not subscribed]... cannot gain access to its content, or grant access to others, unless specifically permitted to do so."

### Discourse

Groups are admin-created, but the **group owner** role delegates day-to-day membership, and groups can be "closed", accept requests ("Allow users to send membership requests to group owners" adds a Request button and a message template) or be open ("Allow users to join the group freely" requires a publicly visible group) ([meta](https://meta.discourse.org/t/configuring-group-access-membership-and-visibility/138296)). Category security is a per-group matrix of See / Reply / Create with the well-known footgun that "By default, new categories allow the 'everyone' group to See/Reply/Create" and that moderators "must be explicitly included in permission rules" ([meta](https://meta.discourse.org/t/understanding-groups-and-category-permissions-security-settings/87678)). Years of meta.discourse requests for members to self-create groups/categories have been answered with "use a group owner" or plugins *(unverified)*.

### Problems to design around

- **Does the member know they are in your group?** Dreamwidth/LJ/Diaspora: no, and no notification. Google+: notified of being circled, not which circle. Instagram: not notified, but the green ring reveals it on first use. Zulip/Discourse/Discord: groups are public objects; membership is visible and often shown on profiles. A forum sits closer to Zulip: groups that gate shared spaces are social facts, not private taxonomies.
- **Symmetric vs asymmetric.** Asymmetric lists are lightweight (Watson) but confuse newcomers and weaken the network effect (HN post-mortems). Forums already have a symmetric *membership* edge, so a one-way "my list of people" has fewer side effects than on a social network.
- **Nested groups.** Zulip's subgroup semantics are powerful but require closure computation and cycle prevention; for 500 members this is cheap, but it makes "who can see this?" harder to explain.
- **Group names leak information.** "People I don't trust", "Exes", "Drama-prone" as a group name becomes a cruelty if displayed. Dreamwidth hides names; Zulip and Discourse show them. If names are visible, membership should be visible too, so the same rule applies to both.
- **Deleting a group that is in use.** Zulip has deactivation rather than deletion ("Deactivate a user group") and blocks deactivation while a group is referenced *(behaviour unverified)*. Discourse groups used in category security silently drop the permission row when deleted *(unverified)*.
- **A user leaving the forum.** Their groups and the spaces that depend on them become orphans; Discourse transfers ownership to admins, Zulip groups persist because they are organisation objects. Google+ "circles" simply vanished with the account.
- **Audience growth through replies.** Watson: users "did not understand re-sharing and commenting, and how it can extend the post's original audience." In a forum, the equivalent risk is quoting or moving a thread across spaces with different audiences.

## 5. Permission UI practice

- **Discourse category Security tab**: a table of group rows, each with See / Reply / Create checkboxes, plus "Add a group". Simple, scannable, and the reference point most forum admins know. Known pitfall: the default "everyone" row ([meta](https://meta.discourse.org/t/understanding-groups-and-category-permissions-security-settings/87678)).
- **Zulip channel settings**: one dropdown-with-chips per verb ("Who can send messages"), each accepting roles, groups and users. Very flexible, but ten independent verbs is a lot for a casual owner.
- **Google Docs sharing dialog** is the folk standard: a people list with per-row role, plus one "General access" line (Restricted / Anyone with the link). Google redesigned it in 2020 specifically because people confused the two halves: it "visually separat[es] link sharing with people versus groups to help prevent accidental changes to link permissions" ([UMich ITS summary](https://its.umich.edu/node/184678)). Even so, a 2023 industry scan found "22% of external data shares utilize 'open links'" ([Valence](https://www.valencesecurity.com/resources/blogs/the-danger-of-sharing-files-with-anyone-with-the-link-examining-a-risky-google-drive-misconfiguration)): a single misunderstood global setting outweighs any per-person precision.
- **"View as" previews**: Facebook's View As ("see your profile as [a friend] would, respecting any custom friend lists, restricted visibility settings") exists because settings and expectations diverge (Liu et al.'s 37%). Dreamwidth has the same affordance informally (log out / use a test account). NN/g's guidance on complex applications recommends staged disclosure (show "Give permissions to" only after "Mark this setting as private" is ticked) ([NN/g](https://www.nngroup.com/articles/usability-heuristics-complex-applications/)); I found no NN/g study specifically on permission dialogs *(unverified)*.
- **Audience indicators on content**: Facebook's globe / two-heads / lock icons; Google+ "Public / Extended circles / Limited" labels, which two-thirds of users never noticed (Watson); Mastodon's globe / moon / lock / @; Instagram's green ring; LJ/DW's lock icon on every non-public entry. Watson's recommendation stands: show the *named* audience (which group/space), not just a count of people, and make it visible at the point of writing a reply, not only on the original post.

## Design implications for Parlour

The literature is consistent: people want to say different things to different audiences (Kairam; Vitak's honesty effect), they understand lists when shown them (Watson), but **per-post selection among many self-maintained lists collapses under its own bookkeeping**, and the failures are almost always over-exposure (Liu). What survives is coarse tiers, a single trusted list, or account/space boundaries that are legible to everyone involved.

1. **Offer two granularities, not N.** (a) Site-wide coarse tiers that every space can use without configuration: *Everyone on Parlour*, *Members only* (if guests exist), *Space members*. (b) **At most a small number of member-owned lists**, with the UI shaped around one or two rather than ten: a default "Close friends"-style list per member, and the ability to create more only from a settings page, not from the composer. Instagram's single list and Twitter Circles' 150-person cap are the proven shape; Google+'s unbounded circle count is the cautionary one.
2. **Make the space, not the post, the unit of audience.** Per-post audience pickers were the G+ failure point ("Do I post to friends? Do I post to family?"). In Parlour the subforum already carries the audience; a thread inherits it. Allow a member to set a space's *readers* and *posters* to {everyone, members, a named list, space members} and then stop. Do not add per-thread overrides in v1.
3. **Treat member lists as semi-public groups, not secret filters.** A forum space is a shared room, not a private journal. If a list gates a space, people inside the space can see who else is in it (as in Zulip, Discourse and Matrix restricted rooms), and someone added to a list is told so, by name of list. Consequently: list names are visible to their members, so the UI should say so at creation time ("members of this list will see its name"). This avoids the Dreamwidth problem of readers not knowing whether they are seeing an "all access" or a filtered view, and the G+ problem of silent circling. If you want a secret list, that is the *Dreamwidth model* and should be a distinct, clearly-labelled feature or omitted.
4. **Keep lists flat and owner-only.** No nested lists, no co-owners in v1. Zulip's subgroups are the right tool for a company org chart, not for 500 friends; nesting makes "who can see this?" unexplainable. Membership edits by the owner only; a "request to join" button (Discourse-style) handles the social side.
5. **Show the audience everywhere, with names.** Every thread and the reply composer carry a small indicator: a globe for public, a lock plus the space name for members-only, a lock plus the list name ("Visible to: Close friends (14)") for list-gated spaces. Make the count clickable to show the people. Watson found two-thirds of users never noticed G+'s labels, so put it next to the reply box, not only in the header.
6. **Provide "View as" and a leaving-the-audience warning.** A "Who can see this?" button on any thread lists the current readers, and a space owner can preview the forum as a given member. When a thread is moved or quoted into a space with a *wider* audience, warn the mover; Watson's reshare problem is the same thing.
7. **Define lifecycle rules up front.** A list in use by a space cannot be deleted, only emptied or renamed (Zulip's deactivate-not-delete). When a member leaves Parlour, their lists are frozen and the spaces that depend on them fall back to *owner-only* read until an admin reassigns (Discourse's admin-fallback). Removing someone from a list should remove their future access but not retroactively hide their own posts from themselves.
8. **Avoid:** per-post audience pickers; more than a handful of lists per person surfaced in the composer; invisible membership; group names shown to non-members; an "anyone with the link" style global toggle next to the per-group table (the Google Drive confusion); defaulting new spaces to "everyone can see/reply/create" (the Discourse footgun). Default new member-owned spaces to *Members only*, with public as an explicit opt-in.
9. **Expect use for relevance, not secrecy.** Both G+ studies found circles were used mainly to avoid "polluting" others' streams. For Parlour this means the personal feed's list-gated mode is more likely to be used for "my D&D group" or "people who want my running updates" than for confessions. Design copy and defaults around interest-routing, and let the privacy use follow.
