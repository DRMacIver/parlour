# Staff visibility of private content and "break glass" access: research for Parlour

Research date: 3 October 2026. Quotes are verbatim from the cited page unless marked as paraphrase. Claims taken from secondary sources or not confirmed against a primary document are marked *(unverified)*.

## 1. What existing platforms do, and how they tell users

### Summary table

| Platform | Can mods/admins read private spaces by default? | How users are told |
|---|---|---|
| Zulip | No (private channels and DMs). Owner-level export can include DMs, now consent-gated. | Explicit security-model doc and help pages |
| Slack | Owners on paid plans can export DMs; no user notification since 2018 | Privacy policy, help centre; press coverage |
| Discord | Server admins: all channels, no DMs. Discord T&S: reported DMs | Permission docs; T&S pages |
| Mastodon | Admins can read DMs on their server (reports or database) | In-product warning, reworded twice after complaints |
| Matrix | Homeserver admin reads any unencrypted room via admin API/DB | Community blog posts, not product UI |
| Discourse | Admins: any PM via profile. Moderators: only flagged PMs | Discourse Meta threads; optional staff-action log |
| Reddit | Admins yes (reported chats), subreddit mods no | Mod help centre |
| Facebook | Group admins can't read Messenger; Facebook sees private groups | Newsroom posts |
| Dreamwidth | Staff can override security levels; logged and audited | Privacy policy |
| Signal/WhatsApp | No (E2EE); admins only manage membership | Product marketing |
| Lemmy | Admins can read PMs in the DB; public modlog | Community posts |

### Zulip

Zulip is the clearest precedent for Parlour's proposed default. Its security model documentation says organisation owners and administrators "can see and modify most aspects of a private channel, including the membership and estimated traffic," but they "generally cannot see messages sent to private channels or do things that would indirectly give them access to those messages, like adding members or changing the channel privacy settings." Being an administrator does "not generally provide the ability to read other users' direct messages or messages sent to private channels to which the administrator is not subscribed." ([Zulip security model](https://zulip.readthedocs.io/en/10.2/production/security-model.html))

The same document is honest about the two leaks: "Organization owners may get access to direct messages via some types of data export," and "Administrators can change the ownership of a bot. If a bot is subscribed to a private channel, then an administrator can indirectly get access to channel messages by taking control of the bot." It also states that anyone with root on the server has "complete control over the Zulip installation and all of its data (so they can read messages, modify history, etc.)."

The export leak has since been narrowed. Zulip's standard export now includes "private channel messages and direct messages from members who have allowed administrators to export their private data"; there is a per-user privacy setting and an admin API endpoint to see who has consented ([Export your organization](https://zulip.com/help/export-your-organization); [Get data export consent state](https://zulip.com/api/get-realm-export-consents); [issue #31201](https://github.com/zulip/zulip/issues/31201)). A full export without consent on Zulip Cloud requires emailing Zulip support from an owner's address, i.e. a human-gated process outside the product.

Zulip 12.0 (April 2026) added message reporting with the most directly relevant design decision found in this research: "Keep in mind that reporting a message does not grant anyone access to see the rest of the conversation. For example, moderators will see only direct messages that you report, not other DMs in the same thread." Reports go to a configurable private moderation channel with a topic per reported user, and the reporter gets a confirmation DM from Notification Bot. ([Report a message](https://zulip.com/help/report-a-message); [Enable moderation requests](https://zulip.com/help/enable-moderation-requests))

Zulip's stance is largely communicated through documentation rather than UI. I did not find a GitHub issue in which maintainers lay out philosophical reasoning for the no-admin-access rule; the position appears as settled design in the docs and in the consent-based export work *(the absence is from my search, not confirmed exhaustively)*.

### Slack

Slack is the cautionary tale. In April 2018 Slack replaced "Compliance Export" (which required advance enablement and notified members) with self-service export. NBC News (Herb Weisbaum) reported that Plus-plan owners could export "all data from their workspace" including private channels and DMs, and that "Automatic notices to employees will be discontinued. The employer will now decide whether users will be told their conversations are being exported." Privacy attorney Bradley Shear: "Slack is a work collaboration tool, not a private messaging app, and people need to understand that." Paul Stephens of Privacy Rights Clearinghouse: "An employee could conclude that the only people who could see the messages are those in the group when that's not necessarily the case." ([NBC News](https://www.nbcnews.com/better/business/slack-updates-privacy-policy-employers-can-read-private-dms-without-ncna862811)) Fast Company's headline was "Slack picked a weird time to make it easier for bosses to download your private chats" ([Fast Company](https://www.fastcompany.com/40547684/slack-picked-a-weird-time-to-make-it-easier-for-bosses-to-download-your-private-chats)). The lesson: removing the *notification* was what turned a compliance feature into a scandal.

Slack Connect extends this: on Business+ and Enterprise, owners "can apply to export data from all public channels, private channels, and direct messages (DMs) with external people" ([Slack help](https://slack.com/help/articles/115004152843-How-data-management-features-apply-to-Slack-Connect)). Slack's Audit Logs API (Enterprise Grid only) records admin actions but "do[es] not enable monitoring of message content" ([Slack Audit Logs API](https://docs.slack.dev/admins/audit-logs-api/)); it is an admin tool, not visible to ordinary members.

### Discord

Discord's developer docs state that the Administrator permission "allows all permissions and bypasses channel permission overwrites" ([Discord permissions](https://docs.discord.com/developers/topics/permissions)), so there is no such thing as a private channel from an Administrator. Server admins cannot read members' DMs; Discord's own Trust & Safety team can review DMs that are reported, and DMs are not end-to-end encrypted ([secondary summary, thetoolstrunk](https://thetoolstrunk.com/can-discord-see-your-dms/) *(unverified against Discord's own text)*). Discord says its investigators look at "the context in which something is posted" and that reporting tooling now attaches surrounding messages automatically *(unverified: from the PNLY blog summary of Discord's reporting changes, not a Discord page)* ([PNLY](https://blog.pnly.io/discords-reporting-system/); [Discord: How we investigate](https://discord.com/safety/360043712132-how-we-investigate)).

A revealing thread on Discord's API discussions proposed that reported DMs be forwarded to admins of mutual servers so they could ban spambots. Commenter ooliver1 replied: "Did you consider the privacy concerns with simply forwarding any reported DM to all servers?" and noted that "the sender's messages are being broadcast to everyone without permission." ([discord-api-docs discussion #5680](https://github.com/discord/discord-api-docs/discussions/5680)) That is exactly the tension Parlour's report flow has to resolve: who, besides the reporter, is entitled to see the reported private content.

### Mastodon

Mastodon admins can read any DM stored on their server, via the database or when it is reported. The product's warning was reworded twice because users kept being surprised. ClearlyClaire, opening PR #18108: "The current English warning is apparently not enough to convey that server administrators are technically able to access Direct Messages, as users are still surprised after learning about it outside of Mastodon." In the thread, n0toose described an instance where a moderator read private messages in real time and confronted the user about profanity. The eventual change (#18146) renamed "Direct" visibility to "Only people I mention" with the warning "Posts on Mastodon are not end-to-end encrypted. Do not share any dangerous information over Mastodon." ([PR #18108](https://github.com/mastodon/mastodon/pull/18108); [issue #18079](https://github.com/mastodon/mastodon/issues/18079)) Mastodon's audit log (Gargron, [PR #5757](https://github.com/mastodon/mastodon/pull/5757)) records moderator actions and is gated by a "View Audit Log" permission ([Roles](https://docs.joinmastodon.org/admin/roles/)); it logs actions, not reads.

### Matrix

Erethon's "What a malicious Matrix homeserver admin can do" concludes that an admin can read the "chat history of any unencrypted room" and, even for E2EE rooms, reactions, membership, topics and timing metadata; "the admin of a homeserver has a lot of power over their accounts and ... they as users explicitly trust the admin." ([erethon.com](https://blog.erethon.com/blog/2022/07/13/what-a-malicious-matrix-homeserver-admin-can-do/)) Synapse exposes this directly through the admin API (`GET /_synapse/admin/v1/rooms/<room_id>/messages`) ([Synapse docs](https://matrix-org.github.io/synapse/latest/admin_api/rooms.html)). Matrix's answer to the problem is E2EE, not access policy.

### Discourse

Discourse is the most-argued case and the closest to a forum. Admins can read any PM from the user's profile Activity tab; moderators can read only their own PMs and PMs that are flagged (per community answers, moderators see flagged PMs only while the flag is live; admins any time) ([Can staff read other people's PM's?](https://meta.discourse.org/t/can-staff-read-other-peoples-pms-what-about-admins/138950)). The team has refused to change this for over a decade. Sam Saffron (2015): "This has been covered before quite a few times afaik, its by design not a bug" and "You are asking for something impossible. As an admin you can download a copy of the site and query the DB." Jeff Atwood (2015): "Admins have full access to everything in the system. That is the design, that is the intent" and "If you don't trust the admins on a site you are using, you should not use that site." ([Admins can clearly see all private messages](https://meta.discourse.org/t/admins-can-clearly-see-all-private-messages-of-all-users/26794); [Admins can still read anyone's PMs by downloading the database](https://meta.discourse.org/t/admins-can-still-read-anyones-pms-by-downloading-the-database/26799)) dax (2021): "Admins have full access to the site, including PMs. There is a special setting that, if activated, records in the logs when an admin accesses the PM of others." ([thread](https://meta.discourse.org/t/admins-can-see-private-messages-in-users-profiles/19750))

That setting is "Log personal message views by Admin for other users/groups", which writes a "check personal message" entry to the staff action log "any time the content of a PM is visible to an admin that isn't included on the PM" (awesomerobot, confirmed by techAPJ) ([When does it log "check personal message"?](https://meta.discourse.org/t/when-does-it-log-check-personal-message/162285); [Admin Private Messages Views](https://meta.discourse.org/t/admin-private-messages-views/231586)). It is off by default and the log is visible only to staff. Users notice the gap: sdpiowa, "It won't notify the end user, but it does at least leave an entry"; another user (RedBear) called the capability a "disgusting opportunity" and left the platform ([How alert if admin/mods read users PM?](https://meta.discourse.org/t/how-alert-if-admin-mods-read-users-pm/218083)). Moderator Stephen notes a side channel: view counts increment, so "You can't view personal messages without leaving evidence of having done so." Impersonation is logged but "actions taken while impersonating aren't specifically logged," and there is an open request that "The Impersonated user should be notified" ([feature request](https://meta.discourse.org/t/the-impersonated-user-should-be-notified-that-they-are-being-impersonated/116933)).

A small-community consequence: the Freegle volunteer network disabled PMs on its Discourse because "Admin users can see all of them," and discourages staff PMs "to avoid extra channels that admins can see but ordinary users can't" ([Freegle wiki](https://wiki.ilovefreegle.org/Discourse_moderation_and_privacy)). Mittineague's 2014 suggestion of access "on a need to know basis for resolving specific issues, not routine viewing" is essentially break-glass.

### Reddit, Facebook, Signal/WhatsApp, Lemmy, Dreamwidth

Reddit's moderator help centre lists reading other redditors' chat messages among the things mods cannot do (paraphrase; page returned 403 to fetch) ([What mods can't do](https://support.reddithelp.com/hc/en-us/articles/15484345352084-What-mods-can-t-do)). Reddit admins review reported chats; its H1 2024 transparency report gives counts of chat messages flagged by automation and reported by users *(exact figures unverified)* ([Reddit Transparency Report H1 2024](https://40687240.fs1.hubspotusercontent-na1.net/hubfs/40687240/Reddit%20Inc/Content/Transparency%20Reports/RTR%20H1%202024/Reddit%20Transparency%20Report%20_%20January%20to%20June%202024%20PDF.pdf)). Modmail is a separate shared inbox gated by a "manage mod mail" permission.

Facebook group admins cannot read members' Messenger conversations *(unverified; inferred from Meta's statement that "How we treat content and data in Community Chats is different from that of private messaging in Messenger")* ([Meta, 2022](https://about.fb.com/news/2022/09/safety-and-privacy-in-community-chats/)). Facebook itself sees everything in private groups: Tom Alison (VP Engineering, 2019): "Community Standards apply across Facebook, including in private groups" and "We can use AI and machine learning to proactively detect bad content before anyone reports it." ([Meta, 2019](https://about.fb.com/news/2019/08/private-groups-safety/))

Signal and WhatsApp group/community admins manage membership and settings only; content is end-to-end encrypted and WhatsApp states "no one else, not even WhatsApp, can read" community messages ([WhatsApp FAQ](https://faq.whatsapp.com/495856382464992/); [EFF on Signal groups](https://ssd.eff.org/module/creating-and-managing-signal-groups)).

Lemmy admins can read PMs in the database; users are told to use GPG for anything sensitive ([lemmy.eus](https://lemmy.eus/post/194463)). Before 0.19.1, a bug let any authenticated user report another person's PM and receive its content in the API response ([GHSA-r64r-5h43-26qv](https://github.com/LemmyNet/lemmy/security/advisories/GHSA-r64r-5h43-26qv)), a reminder that report flows are a privacy-sensitive surface. Lemmy's distinctive feature is a public modlog: "instance mods and admins can't just disappear people and communities without a trace" ([lemmy.world](https://lemmy.world/post/27463356)).

Dreamwidth's privacy policy is the model forum-era statement: "While maintaining the site, Dreamwidth staff may need to view the contents of your account, including information for which you have chosen a restrictive security level," limited to "troubleshooting and diagnosing technical problems, investigating possible Terms of Service violations, and legal compliance issues," and "Any use of this ability is logged and regularly audited." ([Dreamwidth privacy policy](https://www.dreamwidth.org/legal/privacy))

## 2. Reporting private content

The platforms converge on one rule: a report reveals the reported item, with varying amounts of context.

- **Zulip**: reported DM only, explicitly not the rest of the thread (quoted above). The reporter is told this in the help page.
- **Mastodon**: the reporter chooses which statuses to attach ("You can attach certain problematic statuses for additional context"); moderators see only what was attached, and Hachyderm's moderation docs note that followers-only posts and DMs included in a report cannot be re-forwarded to another instance's report ([Mastodon docs](https://docs.joinmastodon.org/user/moderating/); [Hachyderm](https://community.hachyderm.io/docs/mastodon/moderation/report-feature/)).
- **Discord**: Trust & Safety sees the reported message plus automatically attached surrounding messages *(unverified)*; server moderators see nothing of a DM report, and the proposal to change that drew privacy objections (discussion #5680 above).
- **Discourse**: flagging a PM makes it visible to moderators via the review queue, for as long as the flag is live; this is the only route by which moderators see PMs.
- **Lemmy**: PM reports go to admins with the message content (and were briefly leakable to anyone).

On expectations: I found no survey evidence on whether people expect a DM report to expose the whole conversation. The qualitative signal points two ways. Anti-abuse voices want more context (Discord investigators say context "can change the meaning entirely"; the #5680 proposer wanted whole conversations forwarded). Privacy voices object to anything beyond the reported item. Zulip's explicit "only what you report" wording, and Mastodon's reporter-selects-statuses UI, both put the *reporter* in control of scope, which is the only design that satisfies the sender (minimal exposure) and the reporter (can add context deliberately) at once. Jhaver et al.'s CSCW 2019 study of 22K Reddit removal explanations found that explaining moderation decisions reduced future removals (modelled 20.8% fewer if all removals were explained) and concluded that an "educational, rather than a punitive, approach" improves outcomes ([Jhaver et al.](https://dl.acm.org/doi/pdf/10.1145/3359252)); transparency about *what the moderator saw* is the analogue for reports of private content.

## 3. "Break glass" outside social software

### Healthcare (Epic "Break the Glass")

Epic's BTG restricts a chart (patient request, behavioural health, VIP). The prompt reads: "This patient is restricted. To access the patient's chart, enter your password and accept the BTG warning. An In Basket message will be sent to the HIPAA Security Officer with notification of your access." The user picks a reason code (Direct Patient Care, Chart Audit, Emergency, IT Investigation, Unspecified, etc.); "Unspecified" requires a free-text explanation. Afterwards, "The Information-Security Management team audits Break the Glass events as part of the ongoing activity monitoring program and will reach out to you or your supervisor with any questions." Merely selecting the record without entering is "bumping the glass" and is itself recorded. ([CMU HIPAA guidance PDF](https://www.cmich.edu/docs/default-source/presidents-division/general-counsel/hipaa/hipaa-guidance-btg.pdf?sfvrsn=f1820735_4)) The essential elements: re-authentication, a reason, immediate notification to an oversight role (not the patient), retrospective audit, and consequences up to dismissal for snooping (the latter widely reported but *unverified* here).

### Cloud providers

- **Google Access Transparency** logs every access to customer data by Google staff with "the affected resource and action, the time of the action, the reason for the action, and information about the accessor" (location, employing entity, job category), with a reason type such as CUSTOMER_INITIATED_SUPPORT and a ticket reference. **Access Approval** adds a pre-access gate: "Google administrators request and receive an approval from an authorized customer administrator prior to accessing Customer Data," notified by email or Pub/Sub, with requests that can expire, and an auto-approve path "to address time-sensitive outages" that is still logged. Legal and law-enforcement accesses are excluded. ([Access Transparency overview](https://docs.cloud.google.com/assured-workloads/access-transparency/docs/overview); [Access Approval overview](https://docs.cloud.google.com/assured-workloads/access-approval/docs/overview))
- **Azure Customer Lockbox**: engineer requests JIT elevation; if customer data is needed, "a Customer Lockbox request is initiated" and designated approvers "receive an email from Microsoft"; "The request remains in the customer queue for four days" then expires; on approval the engineer "receives access for the duration specified in the request"; all steps are written to the activity log. Exclusions: "break glass" emergencies "to restore access to online services or to prevent corruption or loss of customer data, or to investigate a security or abuse incident," plus legal demands. ([Customer Lockbox overview](https://learn.microsoft.com/en-us/azure/security/fundamentals/customer-lockbox-overview))
- **AWS** has no customer-approval product; its position is policy: systems "designed to prevent remote access by AWS personnel to customer content for any purpose ... unless that access is requested by the customer, or is required to prevent fraud and abuse, or is required to comply with law" ([AWS data privacy FAQ](https://aws.amazon.com/compliance/data-privacy-faq/)).
- **GitHub Support**: "the owner of the repository will receive an email with a link to accept or decline temporary access. The owner will have 20 days ... If the owner accepts the request, GitHub Support will have access to the repository for five days," unlocked "for up to two hours at a time"; "All GitHub Support staff access generates audit log events"; "GitHub Support will never access your private repositories without your explicit consent." ([GitHub docs](https://docs.github.com/en/support/learning-about-github-support/about-github-support))

### IT security guidance

Practitioner guidance on break-glass accounts converges on: time-boxed credentials ("15–60 minutes") with automatic expiry; a named incident; a two-person rule so "a single compromised insider" cannot use the account privately; and a record "written to a store the break-glass account itself cannot alter" capturing requester, approver, incident, window, and every action ([Anchor Defense](https://anchor-defense.com/articles/break-glass-access-federal-iam/); [Avatier](https://avatier.com/blog/emergency-access-passwords)). Mapping to NIST CSF 2.0 PR.AA/PR.PS and NIST SP 800-53 is asserted by these sources *(unverified against NIST text)*; Microsoft states its break-glass controls "align with NIST 800-53 and are validated through SOC 2 audits."

### Which elements are treated as essential

Across all of these: (1) tamper-evident logging of every access, always; (2) a stated reason, always; (3) notification to *someone* other than the accessor (an oversight role in healthcare, the data owner in cloud/GitHub), always; (4) time limits, in cloud and GitHub; (5) a second approver or data-owner consent, in cloud/GitHub but not healthcare, where speed matters; (6) retrospective review, in healthcare and (by audit) cloud. Public aggregate transparency reports exist at platform level (Reddit, Discord) but I found no product that publishes a *count of staff break-glass accesses* to its own users; this would be novel for Parlour.

## 4. Do moderators need visibility into private spaces?

**For visibility.** The ADL's 2021 study of closed Facebook groups, Discord servers and Roblox groups found that "Hate and harassment thrive in closed online spaces, which lack the visibility and accountability of more open or public ones," that "extremist groups organize on private groups and channels, not in public spaces," and recommended platforms "prioritize user-reporting within private groups" and "suspend groups when they reach a threshold of violative content." It explicitly does not ask for the same strictness as public spaces: "We do not expect platforms to moderate content on private groups as strictly as in public spaces, due to privacy concerns, but we do expect an appropriate level of preventative moderation and tools for groups to moderate content." ([ADL](https://www.adl.org/resources/report/private-online-spaces-pose-serious-content-moderation-challenges)) Facebook's own response was proactive detection plus holding group admins responsible ("If group leaders often break our rules ... those are clear strikes against the overall group").

**Legal duties (UK).** The Online Safety Act 2023 has "no general exemption for small platforms such as niche, volunteer-run forums"; Ofcom has "specifically called out discussion forums and chat rooms" as user-to-user services ([Bristows](https://inquisitiveminds.bristows.com/post/102k11s/my-organisation-runs-a-small-online-forum-is-it-in-scope-of-the-osa)). The LFGSS/Microcosm shutdown of ~300 small communities was the headline casualty ([Techdirt](https://www.techdirt.com/2024/12/20/death-of-a-forum-how-the-uks-online-safety-act-is-killing-communities/)). The illegal-content Codes require every service to have a content moderation function to "review and assess suspected illegal content," easy user reporting, and a complaints procedure, and Ofcom says measures are "risk-based and proportionate" for small low-risk services ([Ofcom Codes](https://www.ofcom.org.uk/siteassets/resources/documents/online-safety/information-for-industry/illegal-harms/illegal-content-codes-of-practice-for-user-to-user-services-24-feb.pdf?v=391889)). Crucially, the duty is to *act on reports*, including reports of private messages; it is not a duty to read private messages proactively. Ofcom "do[es] not recommend services break end-to-end encryption," and the Act bars Ofcom from recommending technology "to analyse user-generated content communicated privately" in the general codes *(unverified: paraphrased from secondary summaries; the s.121 "accredited technology" power is separate)* ([Wikipedia summary](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Online_Safety_Act_2023)). So a design where staff can see reported private content, and can break glass for an investigation, is compatible with OSA expectations; a design where they cannot see reported content at all would not be.

**Data protection.** The ICO's accountability framework says organisations should "limit access to personal data to authorised staff only," "regularly review users' access rights," and "keep a log of user access to systems holding personal data" ([ICO: Access control](https://ico.org.uk/for-organisations/uk-gdpr-guidance-and-resources/accountability-and-governance/accountability-framework/records-management-and-security/access-control/)). Default-deny with logged break-glass is a textbook implementation of this; default-allow for all moderators is harder to justify under data minimisation.

**Against routine visibility.** The Mastodon and Discourse threads above show the trust cost: users surprised that admins can read DMs, a moderator reading PMs live to police profanity, a community (Freegle) disabling PMs altogether, users leaving. In a 500-person community migrating from Discord, moderators are also friends, exes, housemates and rivals of members; the "moderator-as-user" problem makes routine access to private subforums socially corrosive even when nobody abuses it. The Discourse team's "If you don't trust the admins ... you should not use that site" is true of *server* admins (whoever has the database), but Parlour can and should distinguish the one or two people with shell access from the larger moderator team. Zulip's model does exactly this.

## 5. Concrete designs worth borrowing

- **Discourse staff action log + "check personal message"**: a view event, not just an action event, logged whenever PM content is shown to a non-participant admin. Weaknesses: off by default, staff-only visibility, no user notification, impersonation not fully logged.
- **Mastodon audit log / Zulip RealmAuditLog / Slack Audit Logs API**: action logs gated to staff; none notify the affected user.
- **Lemmy public modlog**: all moderation actions public, by design, so "trolls can't make up stories of unfair moderation without bringing receipts."
- **Zulip report flow**: report reveals only the reported message; reporter gets a confirmation DM; reports land in a private moderators' channel with a topic per reported user.
- **Owner-notifying access**: GitHub Support (consent before access, 5-day window, 2-hour unlocks, audit events), Google Access Approval (consent, expiry, logged auto-approve for outages), Azure Lockbox (4-day request expiry, duration-limited access). Dreamwidth: policy-level promise that overrides are "logged and regularly audited."
- **Epic BTG**: reason code, free-text justification, re-authentication, immediate notification to an oversight role, and "bumping the glass" recorded.
- No product found publishes a user-facing counter of staff accesses to private content; the idea has precedent only in aggregate transparency reports and warrant canaries.

## Design implications for Parlour

**Recommended default.** Follow Zulip: moderators and admins see private subforums, group-restricted feeds and DMs only if they are members or participants. Show this in the UI where a private space is created ("Moderators cannot read this space unless someone in it reports a post, or they break glass, which you will be told about") and in a short public access policy modelled on Dreamwidth's paragraph. State separately and honestly that the one or two people with server access can read the database, as Zulip and Discourse both do; do not pretend otherwise.

**Report flow as the primary door.** A report from inside a private space reveals to the moderation queue exactly the reported post(s), the reporter's note, and context the *reporter* chooses to attach (Mastodon's select-statuses pattern, bounded to that thread). Tell the reporter, in the report dialog, what moderators will and will not see (Zulip's wording). Record in the queue which moderators opened the report. Resolving the report should not leave the private content visible afterwards (Discourse's "only while the flag is live" for moderators).

**Break glass for everything else.** When a moderator needs more than the reported item (pattern of harassment, grooming concern, safeguarding, legal request):

1. Require a reason category (safety/harassment investigation, legal, technical) plus free text, and re-authentication (Epic).
2. Scope the grant to one space or one conversation, time-limited (e.g. 24–72 hours; GitHub's 5 days with 2-hour unlocks is a reasonable upper bound), auto-expiring.
3. Require a second staff member to confirm, except where a single admin exists; log the approver (two-person rule; Azure's internal-approver step).
4. Write an append-only log entry that moderators cannot edit or delete: who, what scope, reason, approver, start/end, and every page viewed within the grant (not just the grant itself, the gap Discourse's impersonation log has).
5. Notify the space's members or the DM participants, by default immediately, with the reason category. Allow a *delayed* notification (e.g. 7–14 days, approved by a second admin) only for safeguarding cases where immediate notice would tip off an alleged abuser; log the delay and its justification. This is the Slack lesson in reverse: notification is what keeps the capability legitimate.
6. Publish a public, aggregate counter and a periodic note ("staff used break-glass 3 times in 2026: 2 harassment investigations, 1 legal request"), plus a members-visible list of grant events with scope and reason category but not content. This has no direct product precedent, which is a reason to do it, not to avoid it.

**Trade-offs.**
- *Slower response to harm in private spaces.* Mitigated by the report flow being instant and by an emergency path (auto-approved, like Google's outage auto-approval) that is still logged and notified afterwards.
- *Chilling the reporter.* If break-glass notification reveals that *someone* reported, a harasser may deduce who. Notifications should name the moderator and reason category, never the reporter, and the report path alone should not trigger a notification to the reported party's space.
- *Delayed notification is a wedge.* Keep it rare, second-approved, bounded in time, and counted publicly.
- *Server-admin bypass.* Cannot be prevented without E2EE (Discourse's and Zulip's point). Make it cultural and procedural: name the people with shell access, state that database reads outside the break-glass flow are a policy violation, and consider logging DB-level access tooling too.
- *Legal exposure.* The OSA requires acting on reports of illegal content including in private messaging; a report-first design satisfies that. Record how reports of private content were handled, since the Codes expect documented moderation processes.
- *Small-team overhead.* With a handful of moderators, the second-approver rule may stall at 2 a.m. Allow single-approver emergency use with mandatory retrospective review by another admin within 7 days (Epic's post-hoc audit), and make the review itself a logged event.

The core argument for this design is the historical record above: every platform that quietly allowed staff to read private content (Slack 2018, Mastodon pre-2022, Discourse) generated user shock when it was discovered, while the platforms that made access rare, logged, consented or notified (Zulip, GitHub Support, Google, Dreamwidth) are cited approvingly. Parlour's distinctive opportunity is to combine Zulip's default with GitHub/Google-style notification and Lemmy-style public accountability.
