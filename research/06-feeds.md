# "Feeds" / personal channels inside a community: research notes for Parlour

Research date: 2026-10-03. Sources were fetched directly and quoted from the saved text; anything I could not open or confirm is marked *(unverified)*. The web-search budget ran out before I could look at Tumblr ask culture, Bluesky feeds, or Metafilter in any depth, so those are thin.

## 1. The primary source: Tasshin's "Feeds" report

URL: https://tasshin.com/blog/feeds-an-anthropological-report-on-a-powerful-online-social-technology/ (Tasshin Fogleman; the post is undated on the page, but it embeds a tweet from March 2022.)

### The community
"This community has nearly 100 people, approximately 40% of whom are active on a day-to-day, week-to-week basis." Communication is "primarily asynchronous", on paid Slack. Parlour at ~500 members is five times larger; everything below should be read with that scale gap in mind.

### Concrete mechanics
- **Channel abundance is the precondition.** "There is no reason to constrain how many channels exist, so users are encouraged to create public or private channels on demand for any number of needs. Users are welcome to join and leave any channel as desired."
- **One channel per person, named for them:** "'feeds' for each person – e.g. #feed-tasshin, where I might post reports about my life that might be relevant or interesting for the larger group. People can respond or interact as they like, providing encouraging support or discerning advice."
- **Owner sovereignty:** "the person whose feed it is has power over that channel, in terms of deciding who to invites, what the norms are, etc."
- **Public and private feeds coexist.** "People also commonly have private feeds that share more intimate details and reflections with a subsection of the community made up of close, trusted friends."
- **Sub-feeds by topic:** "#feed-tasshin-writing, #feed-tasshin-drawing, or #feed-tasshin-dreams."
- **Feeds sit alongside topic channels** ("#disc-meditation or #disc-video-games") in the same workspace, distinguished only by naming prefix. "Users create a feed for each person, or topic that feels alive."
- **Reactions as the low-cost participation layer:** "The culture encourages the abundant use of react emojis to participate in conversations, even if you don't say anything to add to the conversation. Using reactions constitutes a form of reply game."
- **Tooling dependencies:** "full-history access, the ability to make custom channel groupings, and custom emoji uploads."

### Why it produces intimacy (the author's account)
"Over weeks, months, and years of reading each other's feeds, you get a sense of who someone is very deeply, develop a love and affection for each other, and build a shared sense of intimacy in community." The nearest comparison is Twitter's "dark timeline" of locked accounts, but feeds beat it on two counts: deeper threading, and "a clear container for community, whereas the dark timeline is made up of a web of opt-in, one-to-one relationships". The feed is also a refuge: "a safe haven from some of the problems of public Twitter use: bad replies, being ratioed or main charactered, etc. People can feel comfortable being more casual and relaxed".

A norm worth noting: private insight is expected to be re-exported. "Users also often crosspost wisdom that they've discerned from their private reflections and conversations into a public venue."

### Problems the author names
- Async intimacy without practical support: "People can know and love each other deeply, but be limited in their ability or willingness to provide a host of forms of practical support that would be normal and expected in local, in-person relationships". He pairs feeds with Microsolidarity-style synchronous crew meetings as the complement.
- **Discord's admin-visibility problem** when porting the pattern: "the server owner is automatically added to all private channels, so there is no real privacy for users who might want to create a channel that is just for them or a few select friends. It's possible to approximate this if the server administrator mutes the channel, but that requires a shared sense of trust". This is directly relevant to Parlour: if a feed owner restricts visibility, do admins see it?
- Discord's threading is weaker and it lacks custom channel groupings; being logged into many servers at once produces "information overload, context blurring, and a feeling of overwhelm."
- He explicitly flags Zulip's threading model as the interesting alternative.

### Reception
I could find no Hacker News thread, Substack response or blog reply discussing this article (site-searched HN; nothing). Twitter/X discussion is not searchable from here *(unverified)*. The companion post "A Guide to Actually Enjoying Discord" (https://tasshin.com/blog/a-guide-to-actually-enjoying-discord/) is mostly notification hygiene, which is itself telling: feeds generate so much low-priority traffic that the author's main advice is mute, collapse, "mark as read", and "Only @mentions is a good option for most servers." It also quotes David MacIver: "the key to remembering that Discord exists is having some specific discord that is home and that constantly draws you back to it."

## 2. The same pattern in other communities

### Recurse Center: checkins on Zulip (topic-per-person, not channel-per-person)
RC's model (https://www.recurse.com/blog/112-how-rc-uses-zulip, Nick Bergson-Shilcock, 2017) puts all personal updates in one stream, with a bot creating the daily container: "Every day at 12:30am GMT, a bot called 'Alumni Bot' starts a new topic for that day's checkins." The current-batch stream evolved differently: per the RC manual (https://www.recurse.com/manual), "during your first week you'll get your own checkins topic", i.e. it is **auto-created for every participant**, and the manual tells newcomers to read it to de-fear the place: "Try reading the small questions and checkins channels on Zulip to see how often Recursers ask questions and how generously everyone helps each other".

Participant testimony (Jacob Vosmaer, 2024, https://blog.jacobvosmaer.nl/0023-recurse-reflection/): "Each current participant has their own topic where they post status updates about what they are working on or thinking about. Writing status updates is like journaling and gives you a sense of progress. Because others can read your checkins and respond, you can get help, advice, moral support, or invitations to collaborate you might not have thought to ask for." He adds the key cost: "It takes some time each day but it pays off in the connections you gain with others."

The alumni version tolerates very slow cadence. From Zulip's RC case study (https://zulip.com/case-studies/recurse-center/, alum "John"): "each alum uses a dedicated topic to post updates... Some alums drop by weekly, while others might come around once a year. 'You can leave a note, and it's OK if your friend reads it a few months later... Compare that with Slack, where if someone doesn't see a message in some channel on that day, they'll never see it.'" That is the strongest argument I found for a forum/thread model over chat for feeds: a quiet feed is not a dead feed if the medium does not punish lateness.

### X-Team: auto-created #journal- channels with invited readers
https://x-team.com/magazine/why-its-important-to-keep-a-slack-journal (Thomas De Moor, 2023). "When you join X-Team, we create a Slack channel for you that's usually called something like #journal-name_lastname." Audience is **invite-based** ("the people who you've invited to your channel"), use is "not mandatory... but we highly suggest you do", and the framing is explicitly protective: "Your Slack journal is a safe space. Those invited to your journal always have your back". Onboarding advice: "First off, don't be nervous. Your Slack journal really is yours... For your very first post, introduce yourself." This is the closest documented precedent for auto-provisioned personal channels; note it is a company, so the "who gets invited" question is simpler than in a social community.

### Rands Leadership Slack, Interintellect, Microsolidarity, MAPLE
Rands (https://randsinrepose.com/archives/lessons-from-the-first-5000/) runs on "channels are free. If a member wants to create a channel, go for it" (361 public channels at 5,000 members, 800+ at 30k) but I found no evidence of per-person feeds there. I found nothing on Interintellect, Microsolidarity or the Monastic Academy using feeds beyond Tasshin's own recommendation *(unverified)*.

## 3. Prior art: platforms where each person owns a space inside a shared world

### LiveJournal / Dreamwidth
The mechanics are the most mature version of what Parlour is contemplating.
- **Per-entry security**: Public / Access-list / Private / Custom filter (DW FAQ 54, https://www.dreamwidth.org/support/faqbrowse?faqid=54). LJ allowed up to 30 custom groups; deleting a group silently makes its entries owner-only (LJ FAQ 102).
- **Dreamwidth's decisive fix was splitting "friend" into two relations** (DW FAQ 124, https://www.dreamwidth.org/support/faqbrowse?faqid=124): "Subscribing to someone does not let them see any of your locked entries... When you give someone access to your journal, you give them access to read your locked (protected) entries." Users valued this as the point, not a cosmetic: marcmagus (2009, https://marcmagus.livejournal.com/113333.html): "so you can track the entries of somebody without giving them trust permission to your private entries, or give somebody trust permission to view your private entries without having to see all of their entries." He also wanted reader-side **tag filters** to kill the folk practice of "I have these filters, let me know if you want on/off them... a horrid hack".
- **Why it reduced drama**: a Dreamwidth tutorial for Tumblr users (https://dreamwidth-help.tumblr.com/post/132011409667/) explains access is "a way to establish levels of contact/boundaries that was meant to mitigate 'unfriending' drama that was something of a problem on LiveJournal... and, from my experience, seems to have worked well." Etiquette: "it's probably polite to introduce yourself... Whether you want to grant them access is up to you; whether they grant you access is up to them."
- **The reading page** (friends page) was the integration point: every journal you subscribe to merges into one chronological page. This is the "do feeds mix with topic channels on the front page" question answered in the affirmative, at the reader's option.
- **What happens when someone leaves** was contested. A 2007 LJ suggestion to let deleting users wipe all their comments elsewhere (https://suggestions.livejournal.com/737448.html) was broadly opposed: dandelion: "I hate it when other people's replies to my entries disappear"; azurelunatic proposed "mass-screening the comments of a deleted user... the discussion would still be intact for the journal owner"; another commenter: "it's like millions of interconnected sites and also like one big site. I don't think I should be able to wipe out all the comments I've made on all the other journals". LJ eventually made hiding comments elsewhere an opt-in checkbox at deletion, with a 30-day undelete window (search summary of LJ FAQ 16; *details unverified*).

### Facebook Wall to Timeline
The Wall is the cautionary tale for "others can post in your space". Taylor Lorenz, Mic, 2017 (https://www.mic.com/articles/176599/): "Wall posts were an outlet for inside jokes, heartfelt messages, ASCII art, silly reminders, flirting and (naturally) drama." Then: "Posting on someone's Wall is seen by many as weird, invasive and sort of passé. With so many other, more private ways of messaging, Wall posting has become more performative and less personal." Quoted users: "The Wall is the average person's only personally curated space online, so posting on someone's Wall is almost always a faux pas — unless you have a close personal relationship with them" (Sarah); "For him to write on my Facebook Wall seems performative, like it's for other people. It's like, 'I have a claim on you.'" (Zoe). The Timeline change made old exchanges browsable, which "led some users — myself included — to lock down their privacy controls and limit who could post on their pages." Facebook itself attributed the decline in personal posting to "context collapse": "What's the point of writing on a friend's Timeline or posting a status update when it won't be seen?" The intimate function migrated to Groups. Lesson: a space where **others** can write top-level posts about you is fragile; it only feels good when the audience is small and known.

### Cohost (2022-2025)
Cohost shows what removing metrics and firehoses does to posting anxiety, and what it fails to fix. From "Saying goodbye to cohost" (https://meow.garden/goodbye-cohost/, 2024): "being able to freely like and comment on posts without worrying about how it might influence some algorithm was a breath of fresh air"; "The lack of following / follower counts in particular meant that there were fewer reasons to follow someone, or to continue following them... rather than maintaining social links out of a sense of obligation (or perhaps fear), you were free to curate your feed as you saw fit." On being disagreed with: "cohost gave the user who responded the space to write a thoughtful, nuanced response, in an box the same size as mine... It was a remarkable interaction precisely due to how unremarkable it was." But: "ratioing on cohost was just as much of a sport as it was on Twitter — it just assumed a different form... a whisper network of vague, untagged contrary takes, each with its own veneer of plausible deniability... it made it harder to articulate what was actually failing." HN user Twisol (https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=41492807): "There's no global firehose that your posts automatically get dropped into. I'm a generally shy person... Mastodon instances also have a default instance firehose; I'm told people don't really look at that, but the fact that it's there at all makes me overthink posting, and I end up not posting at all." rebane2001, same thread: "It's not addicting, I can check it once a day, or once a week, and feel caught up".

### Mastodon local timelines and Twitter's dark timeline
"Place, Language, and Craft" (https://www.federatedmind.com/mastodon-community-texture/): "On mastodon.scot with a few thousand members, the local timeline is the community. You can actually read it... when you know your audience is a few thousand people who share a geography, you write for that audience." And: "General instances are on-ramps... calibrated for no one in particular." A ~500-person forum is well inside the range where a single merged front page remains readable.

Tasshin's Twitter guide (https://tasshin.com/blog/a-guide-to-twitter/) documents the leakage hazard of mixed-visibility spaces: "if a locked account you are following replies to an account that you don't have permission to tweet, you can see their replies but not what they are replying to... If your account is not locked, remember that your replies to locked accounts are broadly visible. Reply in a considerate way, being sufficiently vague so private context doesn't escape into public spaces." Any Parlour design where a restricted feed can be quoted, linked or notified into a public one will recreate this.

### Tumblr asks
Search summaries describe asks as "a way to let readers start conversations you then publish", with anonymous asks the main harassment vector that owners frequently disable *(unverified; I could not fetch primary sources)*. The design shape is nonetheless instructive: the **owner decides whether an inbound prompt becomes a visible post**, which is a middle path between "only I start threads" and "anyone can".

## 4. Research findings

- **Nardi, Schiano, Gumbrecht & Swartz, "Blogging as Social Activity, or, Would You Let 900 Million People Read Your Diary?" (CSCW 2004)** (https://darrouzet-nardi.net/bonnie/pdf/Nardi-blog_social_activity.pdf). Audience is constitutive: "blogs create the audience, but the audience also creates the blog." Obligation creeps in: bloggers "continued to blog through low-energy periods so as not to disappoint their audience"; Dirk: "Your friends like look at it and they're like, 'What the hell happened to your blog? I can't follow along with what's happening in your life!'" Crucially, **limited interactivity was a feature**: "Bloggers wanted readers but they did not necessarily want to hear a lot from those readers... They seemed to be holding their readers at arm's length." Comments are "rhetorically subservient... to the main post"; one blogger "thought of comments as a 'responsibility' she did not want." Lara: being introverted she was "too closed to tell people in person that I'm having a bad day, but at some level I want them to know" and the blog "takes away the pressure". Design recommendation in the paper itself: distribution lists per post, and "having the option to alert targeted audience members of specific posts".
- **boyd & Marwick, "Social Steganography: Privacy in Networked Publics" (2011)** (https://www.danah.org/papers/2011/Steganography-ICAVersion.pdf). Offline, "most interactions are private-by-default, public-through-effort"; social media inverts this. Vicki, 15: "a status update, everybody can read... but they're not obligated to read it. Like, when you send a message, it's, 'Oh my gosh, this person sent me a message. Now I have to read this.'" This is the psychology of a feed versus a DM: the feed lowers the obligation on the reader, which lowers the bar for the writer. Teens also "wrote their intended audience into being" via visible friend lists, a signal adults ignored.
- **Nonnecke & Preece, "Why Lurkers Lurk" (2001)** (https://www.cis.uoguelph.ca/~nonnecke/research/whylurk.pdf): 79 reasons; those named by at least half of interviewees included "wanted to be anonymous, and preserve privacy and safety", "had too many or too few messages to deal with... it was easy to forget low traffic groups", "were shy about public posting", "had limited time". Asynchronous groups ran ~75% lurkers. "Easy to forget low traffic groups" is exactly the quiet-feed failure.
- **Burke & Kraut, JCMC 2016** (https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/full/10.1111/jcc4.12162), n=1,910 with server logs: "Receiving targeted, composed communication from strong ties was associated with improvements in well-being while viewing friends' wide-audience broadcasts and receiving one-click feedback were not." A feed design that only yields emoji reactions is, by this evidence, missing the thing that matters; written replies from people you are close to are the payload.
- **Fiesler et al., CSCW 2017** (https://cmci.colorado.edu/~cafi5706/cscw2017_fiesler_privacy.pdf): 75% of sampled Facebook posts were non-public; "content type is not a significant predictor of privacy setting" but demographics are. People set a default visibility and stick to it rather than choosing per post, so **the default visibility of a feed is the visibility**.
- **Strickler's "dark forest" / Rao's "cozy web"** (https://www.ystrickler.com/dark-forests-and-the-new-internet/; https://maggieappleton.com/cozy-web): "everything public is an ad and everything private feels real... When I share in private channels, I'm being intimate with people I care about." The cozy web is "spaces where depressurized conversation is possible because of their non-indexed, non-optimized, and non-gamified environments." A members-only forum is already cozy-web; feeds add a second, personal gate inside it.
- **Marwick 2026, "Broken Connections: Fieldnotes from the Old Internet"** (Social Media + Society) looks directly relevant but was paywalled *(unverified)*.

## 6. Design implications for Parlour

**Recommendations**

1. **Make feeds a first-class object, not a naming convention.** Tasshin's community relies on "#feed-" prefixes plus Slack's custom groupings. Parlour can give feeds their own type with owner-controlled settings, and keep them distinguishable from topic forums in listings and notifications.
2. **Auto-create a feed for every member, but make it inert until the first post.** RC gives everyone a checkins topic in week one; X-Team creates the channel at onboarding and says "it's not mandatory". Auto-creation removes the "do I deserve a feed?" question, which is a shyness barrier (Nonnecke & Preece). Do not list an empty feed anywhere newcomers browse; an empty-rooms directory is the documented cost of channel sprawl. Consider a gentle first-post prompt ("introduce yourself") as X-Team does.
3. **Default: only the owner starts threads; replies open to whoever can see the feed.** This matches Tasshin's practice and Nardi's finding that journal-writers want readers "at arm's length" and dislike comments as an unmanaged responsibility. Let the owner choose from a short menu: replies on/off per thread; reactions-only; and an optional **ask box** (a prompt the owner can publish or discard, Tumblr-style) rather than letting others post top-level into the feed. The Facebook Wall is the warning against guestbook-by-default.
4. **Separate "who can read" from "who follows", Dreamwidth-style.** Reading access is a grant from the owner (access list, with optional named filters per thread); following is the reader's choice and gives no access. This is the single most-praised design decision in the LJ/DW lineage and it was introduced specifically to defuse unfriending drama. Keep access lists invisible to everyone but the owner, as LJ did.
5. **Set a sensible default visibility and expect it to stick.** Fiesler et al. show people rarely vary visibility per post. For a 500-person community that is itself gated, "all members" is probably the right default, with "access list only" one click away at feed level and overridable per thread.
6. **Build the merged reading page, with per-reader controls.** The LJ friends page and Mastodon's small-instance local timeline are the same insight: at a few hundred people the merged stream is readable and is where community forms. Offer a front page that interleaves topic threads and feed posts you follow, plus a feeds-only view, plus a per-feed mute. Default-follow nothing beyond the member's own access grants; let discovery happen via a "recently active feeds" strip rather than a full directory (addresses quiet-feed discoverability without exposing dormant ones).
7. **Prefer threads with persistence over chat semantics.** The RC alumni testimony is unambiguous: feeds survive a once-a-year cadence only if a months-late reply is normal. Parlour's forum model already gives this; make sure "new since you last looked" works per feed and that late replies bump sensibly without feeling like necroposting.
8. **Notifications: feeds should be quiet by default.** Tasshin's entire Discord guide is mitigation. Default to digest or "when someone replies to me" for followed feeds; never default to per-post pings.
9. **Keep reactions, but do not let them replace replies.** Reactions are Tasshin's low-cost participation layer and lower the lurker barrier; Burke & Kraut show composed replies from close ties are what actually improves well-being. Consider making reactions on feed posts visible only to the owner (Cohost-style de-metricisation) so they stay a private "I read this" signal rather than a scoreboard.
10. **Close the leakage holes.** Replies in a restricted feed must stay inside it; quoting/linking a restricted thread into a public thread should be blocked or at least warned about (the dark-timeline problem). Decide and document whether site admins can read access-restricted feeds; Tasshin's Discord experience says an undisclosed admin backdoor destroys the point.
11. **Decide the deletion policy up front and tell people.** The LJ consensus was that a departing member's comments in others' spaces belong to the conversation: hide or anonymise rather than delete, and keep the thread intact for the owner. For the feed itself, offer the owner export plus either deletion or a frozen "archived" state; threads other people replied to should not vanish from their notification history without explanation.
12. **Write the norms down.** Tasshin's feeds work because of culture (react generously, reply with "encouraging support or discerning advice", re-export private wisdom carefully). Ship a default feed-norms text the owner can edit and that is shown to first-time visitors of a feed.

**Open questions**

- Scale: Tasshin's community is ~100 people with ~40 active. At 500, will a merged feed stream stay readable, or does it need the RC trick of a shared daily container plus per-person topics?
- Should sub-feeds (#feed-x-writing) exist, or is per-thread tagging within one feed enough? marcmagus's wish for reader-side tag filters suggests tags plus filters beats multiplying channels.
- Is auto-creation right for a community migrating from Discord, where personal channels were never the norm? An opt-in that is one click from the profile may get most of the benefit with less "empty room" risk.
- How much of the owner's "norms" power should be enforceable (e.g. deleting others' replies in your feed) versus merely stated? LJ gave owners full moderation of comments in their journal; that is the precedent.
- Should the owner see who has read a post (RC's Zulip does not; LJ did not)? Read receipts might reassure quiet posters or might create a new anxiety.
- What happens to private feeds when a member is removed by moderators, as opposed to leaving voluntarily?
- Is there any appetite for the Tumblr-style ask box, or does the community's Discord history suggest DMs already serve that function?
