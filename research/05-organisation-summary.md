# 05 — Organisation: cheap subforums, feeds, member groups, staff access

Research summary, October 2026. The question: should Parlour's top level stop being a fixed, admin-built list of categories and become something closer to Slack, Discord or Zulip, where new spaces are cheap, every member owns a personal "feed", members can build their own groups and use them as permissions, the front page is customisable per user over an admin default, and staff are excluded from private spaces unless they deliberately break glass?

This file pulls together five detailed reports, each with quotes and URLs:

- [06-feeds.md](06-feeds.md): "feeds" and personal channels as a social technology (Tasshin's report, Recurse Center, X-Team, Dreamwidth, the Facebook Wall, Cohost, the CSCW literature)
- [07-channel-organisation.md](07-channel-organisation.md): channel sprawl, discovery, defaults, home views, sidebar sections, ownership
- [08-groups-permissions.md](08-groups-permissions.md): Google+ circles, Facebook lists, Dreamwidth filters, Zulip's group model, permission UI
- [09-staff-access.md](09-staff-access.md): what each platform lets staff see, reporting private content, break-glass patterns in healthcare and cloud, the UK Online Safety Act
- [10-prior-art.md](10-prior-art.md): member-owned spaces in Lemmy, Reddit, Discourse, Tildes, Tumblr Communities and others; front pages that mix personal and topical spaces; caching and permission evaluation

Method and caveats are as in report 02: web searches plus page fetches, quotes pulled out by a summarising fetch tool and attributed to the page and username shown, so check an original before quoting it in public. Each report marks what it could not verify. Two gaps worth knowing: no public discussion of Tasshin's article could be found (Hacker News has none; X is not searchable from here), and Tasshin's community is about 100 people with 40 active, so his experience is five times smaller than Parlour's first community.

## The short version

1. **The feeds pattern is real and well attested**, in Tasshin's Slack, in Recurse Center's per-person "checkins" topics on Zulip, in X-Team's auto-created `#journal-name` channels and, at scale and for twenty years, in LiveJournal and Dreamwidth. A forum is a better substrate for it than chat: Recurse alumni post once a week or once a year and it still works because "it's OK if your friend reads it a few months later. Compare that with Slack, where if someone doesn't see a message in some channel on that day, they'll never see it."
2. **Cheap spaces are viable only together with an aggregated home view and an activity-ranked directory.** Every platform that let members create spaces (Slack, Zulip, Reddit, Lemmy) grew the same scar tissue: a creation gate, a reclaim or archive process, and a lighter unit (thread, topic, tag) that absorbs most "I want a space" impulses. The anti-sprawl advice is uniform, and Parlour's members are coming from Discord, where the guides say 8 to 15 channels for a server this size because splitting people "makes every room feel empty".
3. **Defaults dominate.** Fewer than 5% of users change defaults in general; 92% of active mastodon.social users have zero lists; Facebook's custom friend lists were used by 5% to 17%. Slack-style personal sections will be loved by a minority. The admin default layout is the product for everyone else, so it needs to be designed first, and the standard shape now is "admin owns shared structure, user overrides personally" (Zulip 11 folders plus pins, Discourse 3.0 default sidebar plus a pencil icon).
4. **Member-made groups work when the space, not the post, carries the audience.** Google+ users understood circles and liked them, and still collapsed ten circles into one because choosing an audience per post was "a lot to think about". Parlour's model avoids that by construction: a subforum has an audience and threads inherit it. Zulip (since 10.x) is the only mainstream product that lets a non-admin define a group and use it to gate a space, and its permission design is the one to copy.
5. **Default-deny for staff is the Zulip precedent, and notification is what makes any override legitimate.** Slack's 2018 scandal was not that owners could export DMs but that Slack stopped telling members. Discourse has refused for a decade to stop admins reading PMs and the threads show the trust cost. A report should reveal only what was reported; everything else goes through a logged, time-limited, second-approved and member-notified break-glass. The UK Online Safety Act requires acting on reports of private content, not reading it proactively, so this design is compatible with it.

## 1. Feeds

**What makes them work.** Tasshin names three mechanics: unlimited channel creation, one owner-sovereign channel per person ("the person whose feed it is has power over that channel, in terms of deciding who to invites, what the norms are"), and a culture of abundant emoji reactions as low-cost participation. Intimacy comes from "weeks, months, and years of reading each other's feeds". The feed is also a refuge: "a safe haven from some of the problems of public Twitter use: bad replies, being ratioed or main charactered". His own list of problems is almost exactly the list a forum fixes or must fix in software: Discord's "server owner is automatically added to all private channels, so there is no real privacy", weak threading, and notification overload (his companion Discord guide is almost entirely about muting).

**Who starts threads.** The Facebook Wall is the warning against letting others post top-level in your space: it decayed to birthday messages because posting on someone's Wall came to read as "performative... like 'I have a claim on you'". Nardi et al. (CSCW 2004) found journal writers want readers "at arm's length" and some treat comments as a "responsibility". The supported default is the one you proposed: only the owner starts threads, and whoever can see the feed can reply. An optional Tumblr-style ask box (the owner publishes or discards the prompt) is the middle path if the community wants one.

**Auto-create or opt in.** Recurse auto-creates a checkins topic for everyone in week one; X-Team creates the channel at onboarding and says "it's not mandatory". Auto-creation removes the "do I deserve a feed?" question, which is a documented shyness barrier (Nonnecke and Preece). The cost is 500 near-empty rooms, which is the documented failure of channel sprawl. The compromise: create the feed for everyone but show it nowhere until its first post, and surface active feeds through a "recently active feeds" strip rather than a directory.

**Reading.** The Dreamwidth reading page and Mastodon's small-instance local timelines are the same finding: at a few hundred people a merged stream is readable and is where community forms. But a merged chronological stream over sources of unequal volume is the single most complained-about front page (Lemmy: "2 communities are basically all I see"). Feeds should be one section of their own, with a cap per source, so a quiet friend is never buried under a busy subforum. Nobody has data on whether 500 members keeps this readable; Tasshin's 100 does.

**Visibility.** Fiesler et al. (CSCW 2017) found people set one visibility and stick with it, so the feed-level default is the visibility. For a community that is itself gated, "all members" is the obvious default, with a restricted mode one setting away. Dreamwidth's split of "friend" into subscribe (reader's choice, grants nothing) and access (owner's grant) is the most-praised decision in the LiveJournal lineage and was introduced specifically to defuse unfriending drama.

**Reactions and replies.** Burke and Kraut (2016, server logs, n=1,910) found composed replies from strong ties improve well-being and one-click feedback does not. Reactions lower the lurker barrier and should stay; consider making reaction counts on feed posts visible only to the owner so they are a private "I read this" signal rather than a scoreboard. Cohost shows that removing metrics does not stop dogpiling, it only changes its form.

**Leaving.** LiveJournal users strongly opposed letting a deleting account wipe its comments from others' journals ("I hate it when other people's replies to my entries disappear"); hide or anonymise, keep the thread intact for the owner. Parlour already keeps posts as "Deleted member", which is the right side of that argument. Decide and state what happens to a restricted feed when its owner is removed by moderators rather than leaving.

## 2. Cheap subforums

**The condition.** Report 07's conclusion: "cheap subforums are fine; a flat list of them is not." The two mechanisms that every product converges on are an aggregated, mute-aware home view and activity-based hiding of dead spaces. Zulip's are the gentlest: inactive channels are auto-hidden once you subscribe to 20 or more, and folders containing only inactive channels sink to the bottom. Nothing is destroyed and no admin labour is needed. Discord has no text-channel archive at all, and the feature request has been open since 2021.

**Friction, not quotas.** No product rate-limits creation per member, and Reddit's secret karma threshold breeds folklore. The soft gates that work: a minimum account age or post count (Discourse's TL1/TL2 analogue), a required one-line description, and showing similar existing spaces at creation time (the Lemmy duplicate problem in miniature: "fifty communities named c/Linux in fifty instances").

**Promotion and demotion.** Discourse's heuristic is worth stealing: "when you have an idea for what might become a category of its own, create a tag with that name and use it for a while"; Kane York calls it "a self-answering viability study". The forum equivalents: move a thread into a subforum (Zulip's "move topic"), start a subforum from an existing thread (Parlour's quote-fork is already close), and Tildes' trial groups for the other direction: judge after 2 to 4 weeks, and if dead "move all the posts from it back into [parent] with a tag. Nothing is really lost." A failed space is only cheap if its posts can merge back.

**Ownership as a group.** Zulip 10 made channel administration a group (`can_administer_channel_group`), so a departed creator is a non-event. Slack gives the creator "channel manager" rights (rename, archive, posting rules, add co-managers) with admin override above. Reddit's reclaim rule is conservative on purpose: a subreddit is only requestable when every moderator has been inactive across all of Reddit for 60 days. For 500 people: creator starts as the space's administrator, can add co-administrators, site staff can always override, and inactivity measured site-wide triggers reclaim. Personal feeds are non-transferable and simply go quiet with their owner.

**Namespaces.** Lemmy's `u/linux` and `c/linux` broke federation and follows because mentions and search have one flat handle namespace regardless of URL prefix. Either forbid a subforum slug equal to any username (and vice versa) or give feeds a derived handle; Tumblr's `@@community` prefix is a precedent for making the type visible in the name.

**Keep a commons.** Sean Bonner: "I feel more connected to the servers with just a few channels because I know what is going on and who is there." Keep one or two general spaces that everyone is in and cannot fully mute (Slack's `#general` rule), so cheap spaces fragment attention without fragmenting the community.

## 3. The front page: admin default, personal override

**What the numbers say.** Nielsen: fewer than 5% change defaults. Mastodon 5.0 blog: 92% of active mastodon.social users have zero lists, and one in ten engaged testers did not know lists existed. Twitter Lists and Reddit custom feeds are power-user features. Slack custom sections are paid-plan, private, and reviewed well only by power users. Reddit's 2017 removal of default subreddits shows the other edge: defaults "had the effect of editorializing Reddit" and gave the defaults "a disproportionate amount of traffic".

**The settled pattern.** Discourse 3.0 kept one shared Latest view because "this shared view of everything is an important aspect of establishing a common experience for communities", and moved personalisation into a sidebar whose default is a site setting that users override with a pencil icon; changed defaults can be applied "for everyone who didn't already change them manually". Zulip 11 made folders admin-managed, added per-user pinned channels that "trump any channel folders", and a toggle to ignore folders. Discord's all-admin channel ordering is its most complained-about limitation; Slack's all-personal sections leave newcomers with nothing structured.

**Applied to your proposal.** User-created Slack-style sections are right as the personal layer, and the admin default you mentioned is not a detail but the main thing: it is what 90% of members will ever see, and it is what the anonymous cache serves. Ship it as a stored default layout that new accounts copy and may then edit, keep a one-click shared "everything latest" view, and let sections be either a hand-picked list of spaces or a rule ("feeds I follow", "new and active spaces").

**Shape of a section.** Grouped-by-source views (Zulip Inbox, Slack sidebar) scale to many quiet sources because an idle source costs one collapsed row. Chronological-merged views (Dreamwidth, Lemmy Subscribed) need per-source controls or they drown. The standard fix for the merged case is InfiniteFlow's rule from the Lemmy thread: "show the latest post from every community unless it is over x days old, and only then show the second newest." Make "mark read" scoped to a section; Discourse learned to make dismissal take a selected set rather than all-or-nothing.

**Cost.** A per-user front page cannot come from the anonymous cache, but Parlour already renders every logged-in page per request and the anonymous front page is simply the admin default layout. Discourse caches full pages only for anonymous users and stores an entry only after it is seen twice, to stop crawlers churning the cache; the general ESI pattern (public body cached, private shell punched in) maps onto Parlour's server-rendered page plus a small island for section state if rendering per user ever becomes a cost.

## 4. Member-created groups

**What the research says.** Kairam et al. (CHI 2012): 75% of Google+ field-trial sharers used circles, mostly for relevance ("I didn't want to pollute their stream") rather than secrecy. Watson et al. (SOUPS 2012): people understood circles (21 of 27 described the model correctly) but usage collapsed under bookkeeping. P3: "Do I post to friends? Do I post to family? But what if friends are in family category too? It just kind of got confusing so I just started posting to everyone under friends." Only a third of participants had ever noticed the Public/Limited label on posts. Liu et al. (IMC 2011): Facebook privacy settings matched expectations 37% of the time, and errors "almost always expose content to more users than expected". The coping strategies people actually adopt are coarse: lowest-common-denominator posting, social steganography, and second accounts, because an account boundary is legible and hard to get wrong.

**What did get used.** Instagram Close Friends (one list, a green ring, no notification; "No one has ever mastered a close friends graph and made it easy for people to understand"); Dreamwidth access filters, heavily used in fandom but as a private journal tool where names and membership are invisible; Facebook lists by the 17% whose posts to them were measurably more honest and intimate. One or two tiers get mass adoption, N lists get a committed minority, many lists plus per-post choice collapse to one.

**Precedents for member-defined ACL groups.** Zulip 10.x and Matrix restricted rooms are the only mainstream ones. Zulip's groups are public objects with their own meta-permissions (who can manage, add members, join, leave), can contain groups, are deactivated rather than deleted, and gate every channel verb (administer, subscribe, send, start topics, move). Discourse delegates only membership to "group owners" and keeps group and category creation admin-only; Slack and Discord keep groups and roles admin-only.

**Recommendations.** The space, not the post, is the unit of audience, which already sidesteps the Google+ failure. Offer two granularities: site tiers (everyone, members, space members) and a small number of member-owned lists, with the UI shaped around one or two rather than ten. Treat lists that gate a shared space as semi-public: people in the space can see who else is in it, and someone added to a list is told the list's name, so the name must be fit to be seen. Keep lists flat and owner-only in v1 (Zulip's nested groups suit an org chart, not 500 friends). Show the named audience next to the reply box, not only in the thread header, since two-thirds of Google+ users never saw the label. Warn when a thread is moved or quoted into a wider audience; Parlour's "at most as visible" rule already does this for quote-forks. Lifecycle: a list in use cannot be deleted, only emptied; when an owner leaves, dependent spaces fall back to owner-only read until staff reassign. Default new member-owned spaces to members-only, never to everyone (the Discourse "everyone can see/reply/create" footgun).

**Implementation.** Zulip's production design: roles become system groups, groups store direct members and direct subgroups, acyclicity is enforced on write, and one recursive CTE per request fetches the user's transitive group set, so "the group-based permissions add a total of 1 query". Postgres row-level security is a worse fit (correlated subqueries per row, non-leakproof functions blocking index use). Parlour's `Viewer` already carries `group_ids`; the extension is natural.

## 5. Staff access and break glass

**The precedents.** Zulip: administrators "generally cannot see messages sent to private channels" or others' DMs; its security doc is honest about the two leaks (owner export, taking over a bot) and about root access, and export of private data is now consent-gated per user. Zulip 12 added reporting with the rule "reporting a message does not grant anyone access to see the rest of the conversation". Slack 2018: owners could already export DMs; the scandal was "Automatic notices to employees will be discontinued." Discourse: Jeff Atwood, "Admins have full access to everything in the system. That is the design"; the only mitigation is an off-by-default staff log that users never see, and the threads record users leaving and Freegle disabling PMs entirely. Mastodon reworded its DM warning twice because users "are still surprised" admins can read them. Dreamwidth's privacy policy is the forum-era template: staff may override security levels only for troubleshooting, ToS investigations or legal compliance, and "Any use of this ability is logged and regularly audited."

**Why it matters more at 500.** In a community this size moderators are also friends, exes and housemates of members; routine access to private spaces is socially corrosive even when nobody abuses it. The Discourse answer ("if you don't trust the admins, don't use the site") is true of whoever has the database, but Parlour can distinguish the one or two people with shell access from the moderator team, which is exactly what Zulip does. Say plainly that server admins sit outside the mechanism.

**Reporting as the primary door.** A report from inside a private space should reveal exactly the reported posts, the reporter's note, and context the reporter chooses to attach (Mastodon's select-statuses pattern, bounded to that thread). Tell the reporter in the dialog what moderators will and will not see. Moderators see it only while the report is open (Discourse's rule for flagged PMs). Note that the prototype currently does the opposite: decision 9 in `docs/prototype-decisions.md` makes a reported conversation visible to staff permanently.

**Break glass for everything else.** The elements every serious system shares: a stated reason, re-authentication (Epic's break-the-glass requires both), a tamper-evident log of every access (not just the grant; Discourse's impersonation log has that gap), notification to someone other than the accessor (an oversight role in healthcare; the data owner for GitHub Support, Google Access Approval and Azure Lockbox), time limits (GitHub: five days, unlocked two hours at a time), and retrospective review. A recommended Parlour design: reason category plus free text and re-authentication; scope to one space or conversation for 24 to 72 hours; a second staff member confirms, or a single admin may proceed in an emergency with mandatory review by another admin within 7 days, itself logged; append-only log of who, what, why, approver, and every page viewed; notify the space's members with the moderator's name and reason category (never the reporter), delayed only for safeguarding with a second approval and a logged justification; and a public aggregate counter ("staff used break-glass 3 times this year: 2 harassment investigations, 1 legal request"). No product publishes such a counter; the nearest things are transparency reports and Lemmy's public modlog.

**Law.** The Online Safety Act has no small-forum exemption (the LFGSS shutdown is the headline case), but its duty is to act on reports of illegal content including in private messaging, not to read private messages proactively, and the ICO's access-control guidance (limit access, log access) favours default-deny with logged exceptions. The ADL's study of closed groups asks platforms to "prioritize user-reporting within private groups" and says explicitly it does not expect private spaces to be moderated as strictly as public ones.

## 6. What this would change in DESIGN.md

If you go this way, these are the parts of the current design that move; the rest (flat threads, read markers, quote-forks, invites, the anonymous cache, the access layer) stays.

- **§2 scope**: "There are no subforums", "Groups, managed by admins", and "Admins and moderators can see everything" all change.
- **§3.1 visibility**: categories become spaces with a kind (topic or feed), an owner group, and read and write audiences chosen from {everyone, members, named groups, space members}. The `threads.visibility` copy and the "at most as visible" rule still work; the `groups` case just gets more groups.
- **§3.6 and groups**: groups gain an owner (or an administering group), a visibility rule for name and membership, "deactivate not delete", and a request-to-join flow. Invites still grant groups.
- **§3.7 DMs and §8 moderation**: reporting reveals only the reported items while the report is open; a `break_glass_grants` table and an append-only staff-reads log; member notification; a public counter on `/moderation`.
- **Sections**: `sections` becomes a per-user layout (sections containing spaces or rules) plus one admin default layout that new accounts copy and that anonymous visitors see. `/` for a logged-in member renders their layout; `/latest` stays as the shared view.
- **Lifecycle**: creation gate (account age or posts), required description, similar-space suggestions, move-thread-to-space, merge-space-back-with-tag, inactivity hiding, archive, and a reclaim rule.
- **URLs**: `/c/{slug}` for spaces and a derived handle for feeds, with a rule that space slugs and usernames never collide.

## 7. Decisions for you

1. **Feeds: auto-created for everyone (hidden until first post) or opt-in from the profile?** The research supports auto-create; the Discord-native community may find opt-in less strange.
2. **Default feed visibility**: all members, or restricted by default? People will not change it.
3. **Others starting threads in a feed**: never, owner-approved asks, or an owner setting?
4. **Reaction counts on feed posts**: public as elsewhere, or owner-only?
5. **How many member-owned lists**: one ("close friends") in v1, or several from a settings page?
6. **List visibility**: Zulip-style (members of a gated space can see who else is in it, people are told when added) or Dreamwidth-style secret filters? The report recommends the former for anything that gates a shared space.
7. **Creation gate for spaces**: account age, post count, or none for the first community?
8. **Break glass approvals**: strict two-person, or single admin with 7-day retrospective review? With a handful of moderators the strict rule stalls at 2 a.m.
9. **Notification on break glass**: always immediate, or delayable for safeguarding with a second approval?
10. **Reporting private content**: change prototype decision 9 so that a report exposes only the reported posts while the report is open?
11. **The commons**: which one or two spaces can nobody fully mute?
12. **Scale check**: do you want to prototype the merged feeds section with 500 synthetic members before committing to it, given that the only field evidence is from a community of 100?
